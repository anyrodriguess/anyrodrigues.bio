Beyond cell survival: measuring what neural networks do

How human neural models and MEAs can complement pharmacological and safety studies.

01 / A drug can change function before cells die

Cell viability, morphology and molecular assays are essential, but they may miss early disturbances in network activity. MEAs provide complementary measurements such as firing rates, bursting, synchrony and stimulus responses. Johnstone et al. (2010)

02 / Human neural models

Human iPSC-derived neural cultures and organoids offer additional ways to investigate species-relevant responses. Their usefulness depends on cell composition, maturity, reproducibility and whether the assay is validated for its intended question.

03 / Zika as a developmental neurotoxicity example

Studies of Zika infection in human neural progenitors and cerebral organoids helped investigate mechanisms of disrupted neurodevelopment and microcephaly. Qian et al. (2016)

04 / Organoids on chips

Perfusion and controlled microenvironments can help researchers administer compounds and follow tissue responses over time. A chip is an experimental platform, not proof of physiological equivalence to a human brain.

05 / Could learning be a toxicology endpoint?

Closed-loop experiments suggest a future in which researchers test whether exposures disrupt network adaptation, not just baseline firing. This remains exploratory: robust benchmarks and validation are needed before such readouts can be treated as routine neurotoxicity tests. See Organoid Intelligence.

How do we know if something is neurotoxic?

I find this question particularly interesting because toxicity does not always mean that neurons die. A compound could leave cells alive while interfering with their electrical activity, synaptic communication, or ability to respond to stimulation.

Traditional approaches

Neurotoxicity assessment has relied on a combination of animal studies, conventional cell cultures, biochemical assays, histology, and behavioral tests. Each approach measures a different aspect of toxicity.

  • Animal models: can reveal systemic effects, metabolism, behavior, and interactions between organs, but species differences complicate translation to humans.
  • 2D neuronal cultures: provide controlled experimental conditions and can support relatively high-throughput screening, but do not reproduce the full organization of human brain tissue.
  • Cell viability assays: can identify cytotoxicity, but a viable neuron is not necessarily a functionally healthy neuron.
  • Electrophysiology: can detect functional changes in firing, bursting, and network organization, sometimes before overt cell death.

What changes with human brain organoids?

Brain organoids allow researchers to study selected aspects of human neural development in a three-dimensional environment. Depending on the model, they may contain different neural populations, developing tissue architecture, and spontaneously active networks.

This creates opportunities to investigate how exposure affects neuronal differentiation, migration, synapse formation, network maturation, and region-specific susceptibility.

But organoids are not automatically more predictive. Their maturity, reproducibility, cell composition, diffusion limitations, and lack of whole-body metabolism remain important challenges.

And what could OI add?

This is where the question becomes especially exciting to me. Rather than measuring only whether an exposed neural network remains active, could we investigate whether it still adapts to feedback?

An OI-inspired assay might expose neural tissue to a compound, provide repeated electrical stimulation, record its responses, and examine whether its activity changes across trials.

Potential endpoints could include response consistency, stimulus discrimination, short-term adaptation, network plasticity, and recovery after exposure.

Still experimental: These functional assays are not established replacements for validated regulatory neurotoxicity testing. Researchers still need to demonstrate that changes in network adaptation are reproducible, mechanistically meaningful, and predictive of adverse human outcomes.
