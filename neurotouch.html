NeuroTouch

From tactile sensation to adaptive biological computation.

Research integrity: This is a proposed experiment. Tactile datasets may contain real measurements; any organoid electrophysiology, learning curves or performance values shown in future versions must be clearly marked as simulated until experimental recordings are obtained.

AimBackgroundMethodsBiological systemFuture

1. Abstract and aim

Can living neuronal networks encode and distinguish texture-related temporal information, and can contingent feedback improve their action selection? The project proposes a reproducible computational benchmark using measured tactile recordings, followed by a separately labelled simulation of an organoid-based closed-loop processor.

Why textures?

Touch is inherently dynamic: force, scanning velocity, vibration and contact geometry shape the signal. Robust recognition under changing conditions could support robotic grasping, assistive technology and biohybrid control.

2. Scientific inspiration

Study of tactile input encoding and classification from organoid responses; investigate limitations of external decoders and lack of demonstrated organoid learning.

3. Tactile data and computational methods

Source candidate: Cluster Haptic Texture Dataset. Confirm access, licensing, metadata, material labels and complete recording conditions before analysis.

Experiment A — Material recognition

  1. Inspect recordings, sampling frequency, units, force and scanning velocity.
  2. Plot raw acceleration time series and power spectral densities.
  3. Extract RMS, frequency-band power, spectral centroid and entropy.
  4. Visualize features with boxplots and PCA.
  5. Train logistic regression, SVM and random forest baselines.
  6. Evaluate on grouped held-out recordings with confusion matrices, macro-F1 and uncertainty.

Experiment B — Generalization

Train on selected scanning speeds and test on a held-out speed. Avoid window leakage by splitting recordings before segmentation. Compare performance with an in-distribution benchmark.

Planned reproducible figures

Raw waveform panels · PSD curves · feature distributions · PCA scatter · confusion matrix · accuracy by scanning speed. No quantitative result should be displayed until computed.

4. Hypothetical biological experiment

Human iPSC-derived cortical organoids, electrophysiologically characterized and interfaced with HD-MEAs. Approximate maturation planning window: 4–6 months; detection yield and sample size require pilot studies. A validated slice or organoid–electrode interface may be preferable to placing an intact organoid on a planar MEA.

Closed-loop architecture

Tactile signal → electrical encoder → MEA stimulation → organoid response → fixed output decoder → simulated action → feedback controller → next trial

Compare closed-loop, yoked-feedback and no-feedback conditions. Quantify task performance, response selectivity, retention, transfer and recording stability. Distinguish reservoir decoding from true feedback-dependent biological adaptation. Simulated neuronal results cannot demonstrate biological learning.

Planned analysis

Stimulus-aligned raster plots, PSTHs, firing/burst metrics, network synchrony, PCA, cross-validated decoding, learning curves and organoid-level hierarchical statistical comparisons.

5. From recognition to action

NeuroGrip

Use tactile sensing to select grip-force adjustments in a virtual robotic gripper. Requires additional slip and force measurements.

NeuroGuard

Explore adaptive selection of measurements in a simulated neurotoxicity assay, under human-defined safety limits.

NeuroCulture

Explore virtual bioreactor control from encoded metabolic and environmental signals.

Interpretation limits

Separability of organoid responses is not proof of learning. Improved closed-loop performance must be distinguished from decoder training, input leakage, stimulation artifacts and drift. Clinical or autonomous laboratory use is outside the scope of this simulation.
