BIOLOGICAL INTELLIGENCE / NEUROETHICS

Can a living neural network learn without feeling?

Consciousness in Organoid Intelligence — a scientific question with ethical consequences.

A network of neurons can fire, synchronize, adapt and respond to stimulation. But does any of that imply that there is something it feels like to be that network?

This is the distinction I keep returning to: electrophysiology can reveal what a system does, but it cannot, by itself, tell us whether that system experiences anything. The distinction matters as organoid intelligence (OI) research develops more interactive neural systems.

Where the evidence stands: There is no convincing evidence that current laboratory-grown brain organoids are conscious, and no validated organoid consciousness test. Their biological limitations matter; so does the possibility that future systems will differ. This is an unresolved scientific and ethical question, not evidence of present-day sentience. [1] [2]

01 / definitions.txtCONCEPTS

Intelligence, learning, sentience and consciousness are not synonyms

TermWhat it describesWhat it does not establish
Neural activityElectrical and chemical signaling, including spikes and network bursts.Learning, intelligence or experience.
PlasticityChanges in network properties or connections over time.That the change is useful, learned or consciously experienced.
LearningExperience-dependent change that improves or systematically alters performance under appropriate controls.Subjective awareness.
IntelligenceA contested, task-dependent concept often operationalized through flexible information processing, learning or problem-solving.Consciousness.
SentienceCapacity for subjective experiences with positive or negative valence, such as pleasure or suffering.Human-like thought or self-awareness.
ConsciousnessHere, phenomenal consciousness: whether there is subjective experience at all.That the system can communicate its experience.

These are working distinctions, not universally agreed definitions. Even the terminology used in brain-organoid ethics varies considerably. [3]

02 / electrophysiology.logMEASUREMENT

What can an MEA actually tell us?

Multi-electrode arrays can reveal spontaneous firing, network bursts, responses to stimulation and changes in activity over time. With controlled experimental designs, they can help investigate network adaptation, functional connectivity and learning-related changes.

But a spike train is not a readout of subjective experience. Neither synchronized bursts nor complex-looking activity establish consciousness on their own. Even when neural dynamics resemble patterns observed in brains, the interpretation depends on circuit architecture, sensory integration, task design and the theory being tested. [1]

The experimental problem: If a neural culture changes its activity after feedback, have we demonstrated learning, adaptation, homeostasis, or something else? And what additional evidence, if any, would justify a claim about experience?

For the first half of that question, see my Organoid Intelligence notes.

03 / theories.iniCONSCIOUSNESS

António Damásio: can there be feeling without a body?

In Sensing, feeling and consciousness (2024), António and Hanna Damásio argue that consciousness is grounded in homeostatic feelings and the subjectivity they generate. Their related account describes the continuous interplay of interoceptive feelings, sensory images and a perspective on those images. This is a biologically explicit hypothesis, not a universally accepted definition of consciousness. [9] [10]

“consciousness which is based on homeostatic feelings”

— António & Hanna Damásio, Sensing, feeling and consciousness (2024), abstract. [9]

The implication for OI is provocative. An organoid is living neural tissue, but it does not regulate a complete organism, receive ordinary body-wide interoceptive signals or participate in the same physiological loops as an intact brain. Does neural computation alone suffice, or is bodily feeling essential? Damásio's framework gives us a reason to ask; it does not experimentally settle the answer for organoids.

Other scientific theories ask different questions

ApproachWhat it emphasizesChallenge for OI
Global neuronal workspaceInformation becoming globally available across specialized networks.Would an organoid have the appropriate differentiated and widely connected architecture?
Integrated information theoryThe intrinsic causal organization of a system and integrated information.How could the proposed properties be measured and interpreted in a culture without assuming that complexity equals experience?
Embodied / homeostatic accountsFeeling, bodily regulation and ongoing organism–environment interaction.What is absent when a neural preparation has no living body?

These approaches disagree about what would count as relevant evidence. A burst pattern, a learning curve or a connectivity graph cannot decide among them. Lavazza and Massimini proposed exploring neurophysiological complexity in organoids, but the interpretation remains theoretical and unvalidated for this purpose. [11]

A philosophical question with an experimental edge: if an organoid processes inputs and improves performance but has no organism-level bodily feelings, what exactly has it learned — and what, if anything, could it feel?
04 / organoid_intelligence.logEVIDENCE

Organoid intelligence is not a consciousness experiment

The 2022 DishBrain study connected living human- and rodent-derived neuronal cultures to a simulated Pong environment through a high-density MEA. The authors reported task-related adaptation under closed-loop feedback, which they interpreted as evidence of learning-like behavior. These were 2D neuronal cultures, not 3D brain organoids. Their paper's use of the word sentience should not be treated as a demonstration of phenomenal consciousness or felt pain. [12]

Organoid intelligence research aims to extend functional investigations of living neural tissue, including learning, memory-related plasticity and computation. Organoids can generate action potentials, network bursts and responses to stimulation, but they are not miniature complete brains. Most lack the organized architecture, maturation, bodily inputs and sensory–motor systems of intact organisms. [1] [8]

Three claims that should never be collapsed: (1) the network responds to stimulation; (2) the network shows controlled, reproducible task-related learning; (3) the network has a subjective experience. Evidence for the first does not establish the second, and neither establishes the third.

Can an organoid feel pain?

Nociception means detecting and processing potentially harmful stimuli. Pain refers to an unpleasant subjective experience. A culture's electrical response to stimulation does not establish either a functioning pain system or an experience of suffering. In vitro organoids lack many organized peripheral and central pathways involved in pain in intact organisms. Ethical discussion should therefore focus on credible biological capabilities and uncertainty, not on describing ordinary stimulation as painful.

05 / experimental_design.sysHYPOTHESES

How could we even begin to investigate the question?

There is no established test that can confirm or rule out consciousness in an isolated organoid. A careful research program might nevertheless compare predictions from different theories rather than treat one electrophysiological marker as decisive.

  1. Define the claim. Is the hypothesis about learning, responsiveness, sentience, or phenomenal consciousness?
  2. Characterize the system. Document cell types, architecture, maturation, connectivity, sensory inputs and output pathways.
  3. Use controlled perturbations. Test whether network responses are reproducible and causally dependent on particular interactions, while controlling for simple excitability changes.
  4. Compare competing explanations. Ask whether a result supports learning or integration without necessarily implying experience.
  5. Keep uncertainty visible. Treat proposed markers as research hypotheses, not validated consciousness detectors.
A useful experimental outcome can be scientifically important even if it tells us nothing definitive about consciousness. Demonstrating robust learning in a closed-loop neural preparation would already be a meaningful result.
06 / voices_from_the_field.txtPRIMARY SOURCES

What researchers are actually saying

The language of OI matters. Learning, intelligence, sentience and consciousness are not interchangeable, especially when researchers are communicating results to the public.

“where sentience and consciousness start”

— Smirnova, Morales Pantoja & Hartung, ALTEX (2023). [6]

The authors raise this as an open ethical question, alongside donor rights, rather than as a claim that their organoids possess sentience.

“We must be alert to any possibility that organoids could develop forms or aspects of consciousness”

— The Baltimore Declaration (2023). [13]

This is a call for anticipatory safeguards, not a report of conscious organoids.

“what would they be thinking about?”

— Thomas Hartung, Frontiers interview (2023). [7]

The question highlights how limited environmental interaction complicates claims about the capabilities of isolated neural tissue.

07 / ethics_of_oi.txtRESPONSIBILITY

The ethics of creating systems that might one day experience

The Baltimore Declaration (2023)

Written by a multidisciplinary group of researchers, the Baltimore Declaration toward the Exploration of Organoid Intelligence called for progress in OI alongside sustained ethical discussion. It explicitly raises the possibility of consciousness, as well as the rights and interests of stem-cell donors. The declaration is a research-community statement, not evidence that consciousness has been observed. [13]

From precaution to practical governance

Lavazza and Massimini (2018) asked whether neurophysiological assessments could eventually inform questions about organoid consciousness. Koplin and Savulescu (2019/2020) proposed tiered protections if organoids ever became plausible candidates for consciousness or more advanced cognitive capacities. Both approaches are proposals; neither supplies a validated test. [11] [14]

In May 2026, the Nuffield Council on Bioethics published recommendations calling for coordinated guidance on sentience-related indicators, donor consent and animal welfare where neural organoids are transplanted into animals. These are concrete governance concerns even in the absence of evidence for current organoid consciousness. [15]

What would responsible OI research look like?

  1. Define the outcome. Do not label a change in spiking as consciousness or pain.
  2. Make functional claims reproducible. Use stimulation controls, blinded analyses where possible and alternative explanations for learning-like behavior.
  3. Document the biological model. Maturation, cell composition, connectivity and sensory interfaces matter for interpretation.
  4. Review emerging capabilities. Ethical oversight should be proportionate and revisited as systems change.
  5. Respect donors and research animals. Consent, privacy, transplantation and commercialization are present-day issues, not hypothetical future ones.
The ethical tension: how do we prepare for a possibility without turning that possibility into an unsupported scientific claim?
08 / questions_i_keep_returning_to.txtOPEN

Questions I keep returning to

[01] If a neural system learns without language or behavior, what would count as evidence of intelligence rather than simple adaptation?
[02] Could a system have subjective experience without the ability to report it?
[03] Would adding sensory input and a body change the ethical status of a biological neural network, or only our ability to study it?
[04] What observations could distinguish competing theories of consciousness in a system that cannot speak?

For now, these are questions, not conclusions.
