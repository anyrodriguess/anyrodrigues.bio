Motivation

During my internships, I became interested in a question that still shapes the work I want to do: how can we obtain reliable functional information from living neural tissue? At DZNE Dresden, I maintained olfactory neural cultures and studied their responses to stimulation using MEAs. Later, for my master's thesis at CRTD, I worked on the practical challenges of recording from adult zebrafish retina, from tissue preparation and positioning to analysing light-evoked activity.

Those projects taught me that a recording is only as useful as the biological preparation and the conditions under which it was obtained. I would like to bring that perspective to neural interfaces, where both the health of the tissue and the quality of its electrical interaction with a device matter. I am particularly interested in hands-on experiments that connect microscopy and culture observations with electrophysiological measurements, and in learning the cell-engineering methods needed to investigate these interfaces more deeply.

How I would set pass/fail criteria. I would first collect baseline data across multiple preparations, inspect distributions and agree on thresholds with the team before comparative experiments. Novellino et al. [1] provide an example of predefined culture and MEA QC criteria, but their absolute cut-offs cannot simply be transferred to a different electrode density, cell model or device.

Work plan

Months 1–3

Establish a trustworthy baseline

Work: Learn the team's culture, interface-handling, acquisition and safety SOPs. Record the same baseline variables for each preparation: culture age, coating/interface condition, imaging observations, acquisition settings, noise, active channels, firing and burst structure. Review representative raw traces and rasters with the team before selecting metrics.

Deliverables: A one-page recording and culture QC checklist; a structured experiment log; a small annotated baseline dataset; an initial report of recurring failure modes.

Success: A second team member can reproduce the acquisition and understand why a preparation passed or failed QC. No numerical pass rate is promised before the baseline is known.

Methods informed by [1–4].

Months 4–6

Test what changes interface performance

Work: With the biointerface team, choose one controllable variable—such as an approved coating, culture timing or interface-handling condition—and compare it against a reference condition. Keep cell model, recording duration and analysis settings matched where possible. Include biological replicates, microscopy and electrophysiological readouts; introduce evoked-response measurements only if supported by the system.

Deliverables: A documented pilot comparison, plots of signal quality and network behaviour, a reproducible analysis notebook or R script, and a recommendation to retain, revise or discard the tested condition.

Success: The experiment yields an interpretable decision, including when the result is negative. A change is not called an improvement solely because firing rate increases.

Methods informed by [2–4, 6].

Months 7–12

Measure stability and make the workflow reusable

Work: Follow selected preparations longitudinally, with scheduled recordings and consistent metadata. Track drift in noise, usable channels, spontaneous and (if available) evoked activity. Separate short-term repeatability from longer-term biological change. Review outliers and failures with biology and engineering colleagues.

Deliverables: A longitudinal QC dataset; updated SOP/checklist; versioned analysis scripts; a technical report summarising performance trends, limitations and proposed next experiments.

Success: The team has a repeatable way to compare preparations and identify whether an observed change is likely biological, electrical or procedural. Findings can guide the next design iteration without overstating their translational significance.

Methods informed by [2–4].

How I would analyse the recordings

I would begin with raw-trace inspection and a documented spike-detection approach, then use raster plots and descriptive statistics to check whether summary metrics reflect the recordings. For each preparation, I would report electrode-level and network-level endpoints rather than a single average. Repeated recordings would be paired by preparation; biological replicates would be distinguished from multiple electrodes recorded from the same culture. Results would include effect sizes, variability and exclusions, not just a significance test [2–4].

For stimulus experiments, I would align spikes to stimulation events, exclude or mask artifacts, compare pre-stimulus and post-stimulus activity, and quantify response probability and latency. These analyses are proposed only if Axo's hardware and approved experimental protocols support them [6].
