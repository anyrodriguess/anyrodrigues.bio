SCIENTIFIC INTERESTS / NOTES FROM THE RABBIT HOLE

Organoid Intelligence

Can a neural network learn something without a brain or a body?

I've spent time recording electrical activity from neural preparations. Looking at spikes and bursts is fascinating, but eventually I started wondering: what happens if we stop being passive observers?

What if we stimulate a neural network, record how it responds, change our input based on that response, and repeat the process?

Could we demonstrate that the network learned something? And what would that even mean?

About this page: My personal reading notes and questions about learning, memory and biological intelligence. My hands-on experience is in electrophysiology; I have not personally performed organoid intelligence experiments.

📖 01 / curiosity.txt THE IDEA

01 / What is Organoid Intelligence?

Organoid Intelligence, or OI, is a research direction exploring whether neural organoids can be used to study and develop forms of biological information processing, learning and memory.

The 2023 perspective by Smirnova, Hartung and an interdisciplinary group of researchers helped establish OI as a scientific research agenda.

What makes the idea interesting to me is not the possibility of growing a tiny human brain in a dish. Brain organoids are not complete brains.

It's the possibility of building experimental systems in which living neural networks interact with information and feedback.

Rather than asking only which neurons are active, we could ask how their activity changes when they are given a task.

[Smirnova et al., 2023]

The question that started it for me: At what point does a change in neural activity become evidence of learning?
📖 02 / learning_vs_adaptation.txt DEFINITIONS

02 / If the neurons change their response, did they learn?

Imagine repeatedly stimulating a neural culture and noticing that its firing pattern changes.

That is an observation. But it doesn't immediately tell us what happened.

The network could be adapting to repeated stimulation. Its excitability might have changed. Synaptic connections might have strengthened or weakened. Or the recording itself could have become less stable.

Observation What it could mean
Fewer spikes after repeated stimulation Habituation, fatigue, adaptation or altered excitability
Stronger response after a stimulation protocol Potentiation, increased excitability or other network changes
Improved performance during feedback Potential task-related learning, requiring suitable controls
Performance retained after a delay Possible evidence of memory, depending on the task and controls

Learning and adaptation are not necessarily opposites. Habituation itself can be considered a simple form of non-associative learning when demonstrated under appropriate experimental conditions.

The challenge is to identify the mechanism and demonstrate that the observed change is meaningful, reproducible and related to experience.

Why the wording matters: A network changing its firing rate is not enough to establish goal-directed learning. Behavioral performance, experimental controls and retention tests can make the interpretation much stronger.
📖 03 / plasticity.log NEUROSCIENCE

03 / Plasticity: how does a network change?

Neural plasticity describes the ability of nervous systems to change their structure or function in response to activity, experience and other influences.

Synaptic plasticity is one important mechanism. Changes in synaptic strength can alter how neurons communicate and how circuits respond to future input.

Hebbian plasticity

A familiar idea in neuroscience is that correlated neuronal activity can strengthen connections. This is often summarized informally as "neurons that fire together, wire together."

Real synaptic plasticity is more complicated than this slogan, but it provides an intuitive starting point.

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity

In some synapses, the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic activity influences whether a connection becomes stronger or weaker.

This raises an interesting experimental question: can precisely timed stimulation protocols reshape network responses in cultured neural systems?

Plasticity in human neural organoids

A 2025 study by Alam El Din and colleagues examined several building blocks associated with learning and memory in human neural organoid microphysiological systems.

The researchers investigated immediate early gene expression, functional connectivity, network dynamics and synaptic plasticity, including responses to theta-burst stimulation.

The results support the presence of important neural mechanisms, but demonstrating plasticity is not identical to demonstrating that an organoid can solve a task.

[Alam El Din et al., 2025]

Something I'd love to test: Could the same organoid be recorded before stimulation, immediately after, and again hours or days later to distinguish transient adaptation from longer-lasting plasticity?
📖 04 / experiments.log 2022–2026

04 / Experiments that changed the conversation

DishBrain / 2022

2D neurons Pong feedback

Kagan and colleagues connected living neuronal cultures to a simplified Pong environment using electrode arrays.

The system delivered information through stimulation and interpreted neural activity as actions in the virtual environment.

The researchers reported changes in performance during structured feedback conditions.

DishBrain did not use a brain organoid. It used two-dimensional neuronal cultures. Nevertheless, it became an influential demonstration of closed-loop interaction between living neurons and software.

[Kagan et al., 2022]

Brainoware / 2023

3D organoid HD-MEA reservoir computing

Cai and colleagues connected brain organoids to a high-density microelectrode array and used their electrical responses within a reservoir-computing system.

The work included speech recognition and nonlinear prediction tasks.

This was an important demonstration of biological dynamics being used for computation, but it was not equivalent to showing a brain organoid learning like a person.

[Cai et al., 2023]

Goal-directed learning / UC Santa Cruz / 2026

mouse cortical organoids cartpole glutamate receptors

This is one of the studies I find most exciting.

Robbins and colleagues developed a closed-loop system connecting mouse cortical organoids to a virtual pole-balancing task.

The researchers first characterized spontaneous activity and stimulus responses, then used electrical stimulation to train the networks.

Training signals selected using artificial reinforcement learning improved performance compared with random training or no training.

They also investigated mechanism: blocking AMPA and NMDA receptors abolished the training-related performance improvements.

One limitation is particularly important: the performance improvements did not persist after a 45-minute rest period.

So the study provides evidence of feedback-dependent, goal-directed plasticity, but does not demonstrate durable long-term memory.

[Robbins et al., 2026]

What I like about this experiment: It doesn't stop at observing a change in neural activity. It tests performance, compares training conditions and uses pharmacology to investigate mechanism.
📖 05 / experimental_design.txt HOW TO TEST IT

05 / What would convince me that an organoid learned?

This is where my electrophysiology background makes me particularly interested in experimental design.

I would want to know what the network was doing before training, how the stimulation was delivered, whether the output was reproducible, and what happened after the training stopped.

My experimental checklist

  1. Baseline: characterize spontaneous firing, bursting and functional responses.
  2. Input mapping: determine how different electrodes and stimulation patterns affect the network.
  3. Defined task: establish a measurable outcome that can improve with training.
  4. Controls: compare structured feedback with random, sham or otherwise matched stimulation.
  5. Retention: test whether the change remains after a period without training.
  6. Mechanism: investigate whether synaptic signaling or plasticity-related pathways are required.
  7. Reproducibility: repeat the experiment across independent organoids and batches.

I'd also want to separate electrophysiological metrics from task-performance metrics. A change in mean firing rate might accompany learning, but it isn't necessarily the learning outcome itself.

A useful distinction: Spikes, bursts and connectivity describe neural activity. Task success, retention and generalization can provide stronger evidence about what the system has learned.

06 / The researchers I want to follow

Lena Smirnova, Thomas Hartung and collaborators / Johns Hopkins

Their work connects human neural microphysiological systems, functional assays, organoid intelligence and alternatives to animal testing.

I am particularly interested in their efforts to characterize plasticity and learning-related properties of neural organoids. [OI perspective] [2025 study]

Brett Kagan and collaborators / DishBrain

Their experiments investigate closed-loop interactions between cultured neurons and digital environments, raising questions about adaptive behavior and the interpretation of learning in vitro. [Paper]

Feng Guo, Hongwei Cai and collaborators / Brainoware

This work explores how organoid neural activity can contribute to reservoir computing through electrode interfaces. [Paper]

Mircea Teodorescu, David Haussler and collaborators / UC Santa Cruz

Their 2026 study combines cortical organoids, precise electrical stimulation, virtual tasks and pharmacology to investigate goal-directed neural plasticity. [Paper]

David Gracias, Qi Huang and collaborators / Johns Hopkins

Their engineering work includes shell-shaped microelectrode arrays designed to interface more closely with three-dimensional brain organoids. [Paper]

The wider OI research network

The field also involves researchers in computational neuroscience, ethics, stem-cell biology, neuroengineering and data science.

I keep an eye on the Organoid Intelligence publication directory to discover work from the wider community.

📖 07 / ethics.txt IMPORTANT QUESTIONS

07 / And what about consciousness?

It's almost impossible to read about organoid intelligence without eventually encountering this question.

Could a sufficiently complex neural organoid become conscious? Could it have experiences? How would we know?

At present, there is no accepted evidence that the neural organoids used in these experiments are conscious or sentient.

Electrical activity, oscillations, plasticity and improved performance on a task do not, by themselves, establish subjective experience.

That does not make the ethical questions irrelevant. It means researchers need careful terminology, transparent experimental practices and ongoing ethical oversight.

Hartung, Morales Pantoja and Smirnova discussed the ethical, legal and social challenges of organoid intelligence in 2024.

[Hartung et al., 2024]

My view: I find the science exciting, but I think we should be cautious about interpreting neural activity as intelligence, sentience or consciousness without convincing evidence.
📖 08 / questions.txt PERSONAL NOTES

08 / Questions I keep coming back to

I don't think the most interesting question is whether an organoid can become a replacement for a conventional computer.

I'm more curious about what these experiments could reveal about how neural networks process information and change through experience.

  • How much does the architecture of a neural network influence its ability to adapt?
  • Could we use stimulation protocols to investigate different forms of plasticity?
  • How does maturation affect learning-related responses?
  • Would human and animal-derived organoids behave differently under identical tasks?
  • Could pharmacological compounds disrupt learning-related responses without killing neurons?
  • How can we separate genuine neural contributions from computation performed by the surrounding software?

The connection between electrophysiology, neural plasticity and closed-loop experimentation is what makes this field so appealing to me.

I want to understand not only what neural networks do, but what makes their behavior change.
