Research interests / independent reading

Can living neural networks learn?

From stem cells and brain organoids to closed-loop experiments and biological computing.

What if a living neuronal network could receive information, respond to a digital environment, and change its behavior through experience? Organoid Intelligence (OI) brings together stem cell biology, electrophysiology, and computation to explore questions like these. This page follows some of the scientific milestones that made that research possible, and the questions that remain open.

Scope: This is a personal scientific overview of an emerging research field, not a description of experiments I have performed. The work discussed below includes both 2D neuronal cultures and 3D brain organoids; they are not interchangeable.

01 / oi_manifesto.ini★

Three ambitions behind Organoid Intelligence

The interdisciplinary OI framework articulated by Smirnova and colleagues in 2023 describes a research program for using brain organoids to study learning and memory, develop human-relevant models, and explore biological information processing.[1]

01 / UnderstandInvestigate the cellular and network processes associated with learning, memory, and human brain function.
02 / ModelDevelop human-relevant experimental systems for neurological disease research, neurotoxicity, and drug discovery.
03 / ComputeExplore whether living neural networks can complement silicon-based information processing.

These ambitions are connected, but none is fully achieved. A neural network showing an adaptive response is not equivalent to a human brain, and a proof-of-concept biological computer is not yet a general-purpose alternative to conventional hardware.

02 / timeline.exe1990s → now

How did we get here?

2006–2007 / iPSCs

Takahashi and Yamanaka showed that differentiated cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), first in mice and subsequently in humans. Human iPSCs made it possible to generate patient-derived neural cells for laboratory research.[2][3]

2013 / cerebral organoids

Lancaster and colleagues developed self-organizing cerebral organoids that reproduced selected features of early human brain development. They also demonstrated their value for investigating primary microcephaly.[4] These models are powerful, but they are not miniature, fully functional human brains.

2016 / Zika and disease modeling

Brain organoids and related human neural models helped researchers examine how Zika virus infection disrupts neural progenitors and developing tissue, illustrating the value of human-derived systems for studying disease mechanisms.[5]

2017 / assembloids

Birey, Pașca, and colleagues assembled region-specific neural spheroids to study human interneuron migration and interactions between distinct forebrain-like regions.[6]

2019 / network oscillations

Trujillo, Muotri, and colleagues reported increasingly complex oscillatory activity in developing cortical organoids. Some features were compared with patterns in premature infant EEG recordings, but this does not establish infant-like cognition.[7]

2022 / DishBrain

Cultured neurons were connected to a simulated Pong environment through a bidirectional electrophysiological interface. Importantly, DishBrain used 2D neuronal cultures, not brain organoids.[8]

2023 / OI framework

The OI roadmap brought together neuroscience, organoid engineering, computation, and ethics in a shared research agenda.[1]

Why not just grow larger organoids?

Many conventional organoids lack functional vasculature, making oxygen and nutrient transport difficult as tissue grows. Variability, incomplete maturation, and limited access to deeper cells remain major challenges. Perfusion, co-culture, vascularization strategies, and organ-on-chip approaches may help address some of these limitations, although they do not automatically make organoids equivalent to intact brains.[9]

For a closer look at the models themselves, this topic belongs on my Brain Organoids page.

03 / signal_monitor.log● REC

From recording neurons to interacting with them

Electrical activity provides a functional readout of neuronal networks. Microelectrode arrays (MEAs) can record extracellular signals from multiple sites, allowing researchers to examine firing, bursting, synchronization, and responses to chemical or electrical stimulation. High-density MEAs provide more spatial sampling, while three-dimensional organoids create additional challenges for electrode contact and access to internal cells.

A closed-loop system goes beyond passive recording: measured neural activity affects what the network receives next. The loop might involve stimulation, recording, decoding, feedback, and another stimulation cycle. This makes it possible to test how activity changes through interaction with an environment.

A question I keep returning to: When a network changes its activity after repeated feedback, what evidence would distinguish learning from a temporary change in excitability?
04 / dishbrain_pong.exe2D CULTURE

DishBrain: When neurons played Pong

In 2022, Kagan and colleagues at Cortical Labs reported an experiment in which approximately 800,000 cultured neurons were interfaced with a simplified version of Pong through a high-density microelectrode array.[8]

The interface translated aspects of the game into electrical stimulation and used neuronal activity to influence the paddle. The researchers compared structured and unstructured feedback conditions and reported improvements in performance during closed-loop interaction.

Important distinction: DishBrain's neuronal networks were grown as two-dimensional cultures, using human stem-cell-derived neurons or mouse embryonic cortical neurons. The experiment was a milestone for synthetic biological intelligence and closed-loop neuronal systems, not a demonstration that a 3D brain organoid played Pong. Nor does task adaptation imply human-like understanding or consciousness.

Why this matters for OI

DishBrain showed how living neural networks can be connected to an interactive digital environment. OI researchers are exploring how related questions might be studied in three-dimensional human neural models, where tissue architecture, cell diversity, and access to neurons pose different experimental challenges.

05 / biological_computing.url↗ LINKS

Biological computing is moving beyond individual experiments

Several groups are developing platforms that make living neural systems accessible through integrated hardware and software. Their approaches differ: some work primarily with 2D neuronal cultures, while others use three-dimensional brain organoids.

Cortical Labs ↗Developer of DishBrain and the CL1 platform, connecting living neuronal cultures to digital systems for recording, stimulation, and experimentation.
Cortical Cloud ↗Remote access concept for experimenting with compatible biological computing hardware through software interfaces. Check the provider for current access and capabilities.
FinalSpark ↗Developer of a remote-access Neuroplatform for electrophysiological experiments with living neural tissue, including brain organoids.[10]

These platforms make biological computing more tangible, but comparisons with silicon computing need careful benchmarks. Energy claims, for example, must include the equipment required to keep cells alive and the electronics that operate the interface. Biological adaptability is fascinating; general-purpose computational superiority has not been established.

06 / unanswered_questions.txt?

What do we still need to understand?

  • Learning: How can experiments distinguish lasting, experience-dependent adaptation from short-term changes in neural excitability?
  • Models: How do maturation, cellular composition, and tissue architecture affect network behavior?
  • Interfaces: How can we stimulate and record from three-dimensional tissue without losing access to deeper networks?
  • Reproducibility: Can results be compared reliably across organoids, donors, laboratories, and platforms?
  • Applications: Could adaptive network readouts eventually complement established functional neurotoxicity assays or disease models?
  • Ethics: Which kinds of neural complexity or experimental capability should prompt additional ethical scrutiny?

For me, the most compelling part of OI is not the claim that a dish contains a tiny brain. It is the possibility of developing rigorous experiments to understand how living neural networks process signals, adapt to feedback, and respond to their environment.
