Can living neural networks learn?

From stem cells and brain organoids to closed-loop experiments and biological computing.

What if a living neuronal network could receive information, respond to a digital environment, and change its behavior through experience? Organoid Intelligence (OI) brings together stem cell biology, electrophysiology, and computation to explore questions like these. I started reading about it because the idea sounded almost like science fiction. The more I read, the more questions I had.

01 / why_this_is_cool.txt ★

Why does this interest me so much?

I have spent a lot of time recording electrical activity from living neural preparations. But I keep coming back to a different question: what happens when we stop just listening and start interacting with those networks?

The idea that a neural culture could receive signals from a computer, respond, and potentially change its responses over time is what drew me to Organoid Intelligence. The 2023 OI paper by Smirnova and colleagues gave a name to some of these possibilities. [1]

I'm especially curious about the space between electrophysiology, plasticity, and biological computation. I haven't worked with brain organoids myself yet; this is where I'm collecting the experiments and ideas I want to understand better.

02 / timeline.exe 1990s → now

How did we get here?

2006–2007 / iPSCs

Takahashi and Yamanaka showed that differentiated cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), first in mice and subsequently in humans. Human iPSCs made it possible to generate patient-derived neural cells for laboratory research. [2] [3]

2013 / cerebral organoids

Lancaster and colleagues developed self-organizing cerebral organoids that reproduced selected features of early human brain development. They also demonstrated their value for investigating primary microcephaly. [4] These models are powerful, but they are not miniature, fully functional human brains.

2016 / Zika and disease modeling

Brain organoids and related human neural models helped researchers examine how Zika virus infection disrupts neural progenitors and developing tissue. [5]

2017 / assembloids

Birey, Pașca, and colleagues assembled region-specific neural spheroids to study human interneuron migration and interactions between distinct forebrain-like regions. [6]

2019 / network oscillations

Trujillo, Muotri, and colleagues reported increasingly complex oscillatory activity in developing cortical organoids. Some features were compared with patterns in premature infant EEG recordings, but this does not establish infant-like cognition. [7]

2022 / DishBrain

Cultured neurons were connected to a simulated Pong environment through a bidirectional electrophysiological interface. DishBrain used 2D neuronal cultures, not brain organoids. [8]

2023 / OI framework

The OI roadmap brought together neuroscience, organoid engineering, computation, and ethics in a shared research agenda. [1]

Why not just grow larger organoids?

Many conventional organoids lack functional vasculature, making oxygen and nutrient transport difficult as tissue grows. Variability, incomplete maturation, and limited access to deeper cells remain major challenges. Perfusion, co-culture, vascularization strategies, and organ-on-chip approaches may help address some of these limitations. [9]

For a closer look at the models themselves, see my Brain Organoids page.

03 / signal_monitor.log ● REC

From recording neurons to interacting with them

Electrical activity provides a functional readout of neuronal networks. Microelectrode arrays (MEAs) can record extracellular signals from multiple sites, allowing researchers to examine firing, bursting, synchronization, and responses to stimulation.

High-density MEAs provide more spatial sampling, while three-dimensional organoids create additional challenges for electrode contact and access to internal cells.

A closed-loop system goes beyond passive recording: measured neural activity affects what the network receives next. The loop might involve stimulation, recording, decoding, feedback, and another stimulation cycle.

A question I keep returning to: When a network changes its activity after repeated feedback, what evidence would distinguish learning from a temporary change in excitability?
04 / dishbrain_pong.exe 2D CULTURE

DishBrain: When neurons played Pong

In 2022, Kagan and colleagues at Cortical Labs reported an experiment in which approximately 800,000 cultured neurons were interfaced with a simplified version of Pong through a high-density microelectrode array. [8]

The interface translated aspects of the game into electrical stimulation and used neuronal activity to influence the paddle. The researchers compared structured and unstructured feedback conditions and reported improvements in performance during closed-loop interaction.

Important distinction: DishBrain's neuronal networks were grown as two-dimensional cultures, using human stem-cell-derived neurons or mouse embryonic cortical neurons. This was not an experiment with a 3D brain organoid.

Why this matters for OI

DishBrain showed how living neural networks can be connected to an interactive digital environment. OI researchers are exploring how related questions might be studied in three-dimensional human neural models.

05 / biological_computing.url ↗ LINKS

Biological computing is moving beyond individual experiments

Several groups are developing platforms that make living neural systems accessible through integrated hardware and software. Their approaches differ: some work primarily with 2D neuronal cultures, while others use three-dimensional brain organoids.

Cortical Labs ↗ Developer of DishBrain and the CL1 platform, connecting living neuronal cultures to digital systems.
Cortical Cloud ↗ Remote-access approach for experiments with compatible biological computing hardware.
FinalSpark ↗ Developer of the Neuroplatform for experiments with living neural tissue, including brain organoids. [10]

I find these projects interesting because they turn biological computing from a theoretical idea into something researchers can actually experiment with. Whether such systems will become useful computing technologies is still an open question.

06 / unanswered_questions.txt ?

What do we still need to understand?

  • How can we distinguish genuine learning from short-term changes in neural activity?
  • How does tissue maturity influence network behavior?
  • How can we record from deeper regions of organoids?
  • Could biological networks adapt to increasingly complex feedback?
  • Could these systems help us study neurological disease or neurotoxicity?
  • At what point might their complexity raise new ethical questions?

For me, the most compelling part of OI is the possibility of designing experiments to understand how living neural networks process signals, adapt to feedback, and interact with their environment.
