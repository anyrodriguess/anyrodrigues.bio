Neurodegenerative Disease Models

How do you study a disease that takes decades to develop in a model grown for months?

Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and ALS affect different neural systems, but they share a difficult experimental problem: the disease processes we want to understand unfold over time and involve interactions between many cell types.

Human stem-cell-derived models offer an exciting opportunity to investigate some of these mechanisms directly in human cells.

But how much of a neurodegenerative disease can we realistically reproduce in a dish? And how can we tell whether our model captures something meaningful?

About this page: My reading notes on disease modeling, stem-cell-derived neural systems, gene editing and functional measurements. My hands-on background is in electrophysiology; I am interested in developing practical experience with iPSC-derived disease models.

📖 01 / disease_modeling.txt THE CHALLENGE

01 / Can young neurons model diseases of aging?

One of the most interesting limitations of iPSC-based disease modeling is that reprogramming adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells changes their cellular state.

Neurons differentiated from these cells often resemble developmentally immature neurons rather than neurons that have experienced decades of aging.

This matters because many neurodegenerative disorders are associated with processes that accumulate over time: protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular stress, inflammatory changes and disrupted communication between neural populations.

Different ways to approach the problem

Model Potential advantage Limitation
2D iPSC-derived neurons Accessible human cell types and controlled experiments Limited tissue architecture and often immature phenotypes
Brain organoids 3D organization and multicellular interactions Variable maturation and incomplete physiology
Assembloids Selected interactions between neural regions or tissues More complex to standardize
Directly converted neurons May retain some age-associated characteristics Different limitations in scalability and cell identity
Animal models Whole-organism physiology and behavior Species differences and imperfect disease reproduction

I don't think the question should be whether organoids are better than every other model.

It should be which biological question each model can answer reliably.

Something I find fascinating: Could disease-associated functional changes appear in human neural networks before we detect extensive neuronal death?
📖 02 / alzheimers.log CASE STUDY

02 / Alzheimer's: can we reproduce amyloid and tau pathology?

amyloid-beta tau 3D neural culture

Alzheimer's disease is associated with several interacting pathological processes, including amyloid-beta accumulation, abnormal tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction and neuroinflammation.

Reproducing these processes in a laboratory model is difficult, especially when we want to understand how they influence each other over time.

A landmark experiment / Choi et al., 2014

Researchers associated with Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School developed a three-dimensional human neural-cell culture model carrying mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease.

The system reproduced extracellular amyloid-beta deposition and abnormal phosphorylated tau aggregation.

When the researchers inhibited amyloid-beta production, they observed reductions in amyloid pathology and associated tau abnormalities.

This was an important demonstration that a human 3D neural culture could reproduce selected disease hallmarks within the same experimental system.

One distinction is worth making: this was an engineered 3D neural culture model, not a conventional self-organizing cerebral organoid.

It also modeled familial mutation-associated pathology, not the full complexity of sporadic Alzheimer's disease.

[Choi et al., 2014]

What would electrophysiology add?

Amyloid and tau measurements can tell us about important molecular and cellular processes. But Alzheimer's disease also involves disrupted communication between neurons.

I would be interested in measuring how disease-associated pathology changes spontaneous activity, stimulus responses and coordinated network behavior.

My question: Can we detect reproducible changes in neural-network function before the model develops substantial cell loss or visible structural damage?
📖 03 / parkinsons.log CASE STUDY

03 / Parkinson's: why model the midbrain?

dopaminergic neurons LRRK2 midbrain organoids

Parkinson's disease is strongly associated with degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, although its pathology affects multiple neural and non-neural systems.

That makes region-specific midbrain organoids particularly interesting. Rather than generating a general neural tissue model, researchers can enrich for cell populations relevant to the disease.

Jens Schwamborn and collaborators / 2019

Berger and colleagues developed midbrain-like organoids containing dopamine-producing neurons.

Organoids derived from patients carrying the LRRK2-G2019S variant showed disease-associated changes, including reduced numbers and morphological complexity of midbrain dopaminergic neurons.

This demonstrated how patient-derived cells can help researchers investigate disease-relevant phenotypes in a human neural context.

[Berger et al., 2019]

Kim and collaborators / 2019

Another study used isogenic midbrain organoids with or without the LRRK2-G2019S mutation.

By comparing genetically matched organoids, the researchers investigated mutation-associated changes and identified TXNIP as a contributor to the pathological phenotype.

[Kim et al., 2019]

Why developmental changes matter

A 2022 study from researchers at the University of Luxembourg compared midbrain organoids with developing human midbrain tissue using single-cell transcriptomics.

The researchers found that LRRK2-G2019S altered aspects of differentiation and cellular diversity during development.

This raises a particularly interesting possibility: some genetic risk factors for diseases that appear late in life may influence neural development much earlier.

That does not mean early developmental changes are sufficient to explain late-onset Parkinson's disease.

[Zagare et al., 2022]

What I'd like to investigate: How do genetic changes associated with Parkinson's affect the electrical maturation and network behavior of dopaminergic neural models?
📖 04 / als.log CASE STUDY

04 / ALS: what happens when the neuron can no longer control muscle?

motor neurons neuromuscular junction C9orf72

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS, affects motor neurons and leads to progressive muscle weakness.

A motor neuron culture can help researchers investigate cellular stress, gene expression and disease-associated pathology.

But motor neurons do not work in isolation. Their ability to communicate with skeletal muscle is essential to normal motor function.

Sensorimotor organoids / 2021

A study published in Nature Communications generated human sensorimotor organoids containing neural and skeletal-muscle components.

The researchers demonstrated functional neuromuscular junctions and investigated models derived from ALS-associated genetic backgrounds.

Disease-associated impairment could be detected through muscle contraction and neuromuscular-junction measurements.

This is what makes multicellular disease models so interesting: the experimental outcome can reflect communication between different tissue components.

[Sensorimotor organoids, 2021]

C9orf72 neuromuscular organoids / 2024

Another study generated neuromuscular organoids from patients with C9orf72-associated ALS and genetically matched controls.

The models displayed neuromuscular defects, including impaired contraction, denervation-related changes and disease-associated molecular abnormalities.

The researchers also investigated the compound GSK2606414 and reported improvements in selected functional and molecular endpoints.

These are preclinical experimental findings, not evidence that the compound is an effective treatment for patients.

[C9orf72 ALS study, 2024]

What interests me: Could combining motor-neuron electrophysiology with neuromuscular contraction measurements help distinguish defects in neuronal activity from defects in communication at the neuromuscular junction?
📖 05 / genetics.exe GENE EDITING

05 / How do we know whether a mutation caused the phenotype?

Patient-derived iPSCs allow researchers to investigate disease-associated phenotypes in a human genetic background.

But comparing cells from two unrelated people introduces a difficult problem: their genomes differ in many ways beyond the mutation of interest.

Isogenic controls

Genome editing technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 can be used to correct a disease-associated variant or introduce it into a matched cell line.

This creates genetically matched experimental groups that differ at a defined genetic site.

Isogenic comparisons can strengthen causal interpretation, although editing artifacts, clonal effects and other experimental variables still require controls.

Why I find this exciting

Imagine recording the same electrophysiological parameters in two matched neural models: one carrying a disease-associated mutation and one in which that mutation has been corrected.

If the phenotype changes consistently with the genetic manipulation, we have stronger evidence connecting the variant to the observed biology.

A skill I'd love to develop: Working with iPSC differentiation and genome editing so I can connect genetic mechanisms to functional neural phenotypes.
📖 06 / functional_readouts.txt ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY

06 / Can electrophysiology reveal disease before neurons die?

Molecular and imaging assays can reveal protein aggregation, cellular identity and structural abnormalities.

Electrophysiology adds a different perspective: what is the neural system actually doing?

Questions we could investigate

  • Does spontaneous firing change as disease-associated pathology develops?
  • Are bursting patterns or stimulus responses altered?
  • Do neural populations become less coordinated?
  • Can a pharmacological intervention restore a measurable functional response?
  • Do functional abnormalities appear before extensive cell loss?

MEAs are particularly interesting because they can support repeated recordings over time without destroying the tissue.

But a firing-rate difference does not automatically establish neurodegeneration. Developmental stage, cell composition, network density and culture conditions can also influence activity.

Why multimodal experiments matter: Combining electrophysiology with molecular characterization, imaging, viability measurements and appropriate controls can provide a more complete interpretation than any single assay alone.

I explore compound-induced functional changes separately on my Neuropharmacology & Neurotoxicity page.

📖 08 / personal_notes.txt MY QUESTIONS

08 / The kind of research I'd love to do

My experience in neural electrophysiology has made me particularly interested in the relationship between cellular changes and network function.

I would love to work with human stem-cell-derived neural models and learn how to generate, characterize and experimentally manipulate disease-relevant cell populations.

I'm especially interested in combining genetic manipulation, pharmacology and electrophysiology to investigate mechanisms of neural dysfunction.

For me, the exciting part isn't simply reproducing a disease marker. It's understanding why that marker appears, what it changes in the neural system, and whether we can meaningfully intervene.

My bigger question: Can human neural models help us identify early, functionally meaningful disease mechanisms that would otherwise be difficult to study?
