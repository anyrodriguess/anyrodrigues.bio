Neurological Disease Models

What human-derived neural models can and cannot tell us

From patient-derived cells to disease mechanisms and functional readouts.

About this page: An independent overview of a scientific topic I am interested in, not a claim that I have performed all the research discussed here.

01 / Why human-derived models?

Patient-derived iPSCs can carry a donor’s genetic background into neural cell models. This enables researchers to investigate cellular phenotypes relevant to neurological disorders, while recognizing that cultured models do not reproduce a whole patient or brain. [1]

02 / Disease-relevant phenotypes

Researchers can examine changes in neuronal differentiation, protein aggregation, cellular stress, synapse formation and network activity. Different models are appropriate for different biological questions; a disease label alone does not establish model validity.

03 / Organoids and assembloids

Three-dimensional cultures can introduce aspects of tissue organization and interactions between cell types. Region-specific assembloids are useful for studying migration and circuit development, particularly in developmental disease research. [2]

04 / Functional validation

Electrophysiology adds evidence about whether neural networks fire, synchronize and respond to perturbation. A disease model becomes more informative when molecular and cellular findings are supported by reproducible functional phenotypes.

05 / What remains difficult?

Aging, vascularization, immune interactions, long-range connectivity and variability are ongoing challenges. For drug exposure and safety assessment, continue to Neuropharmacology & Neurotoxicity.
