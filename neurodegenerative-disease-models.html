Neurological Disease Models

What human-derived neural models can and cannot tell us

From patient-derived cells to disease mechanisms and functional readouts.

About this page: An independent overview of a scientific topic I am interested in, not a claim that I have performed all the research discussed here.

01 / Why human-derived models?

Patient-derived iPSCs can carry a donor’s genetic background into neural cell models. This enables researchers to investigate cellular phenotypes relevant to neurological disorders, while recognizing that cultured models do not reproduce a whole patient or brain. Shi et al. (2017)

02 / Disease-relevant phenotypes

Researchers can examine changes in neuronal differentiation, protein aggregation, cellular stress, synapse formation and network activity. Different models are appropriate for different biological questions; a disease label alone does not establish model validity.

Different diseases, different experimental questions

Model / conditionWhat a researcher might measure
Alzheimer’s diseaseAmyloid-β and tau-related pathology, synaptic function, inflammatory responses and network activity; age-associated features are difficult to recapitulate.
Parkinson’s diseaseDopaminergic neuron vulnerability, mitochondrial function, α-synuclein-related phenotypes and altered circuit function.
ALSMotor-neuron survival, axonal integrity, neuron–glia interactions and neuromuscular function in relevant co-cultures.
Rett syndromeMECP2-dependent changes in neural development, gene regulation and neuronal/network phenotypes.

A useful model needs a defined biological question and appropriate controls, not just a patient-derived cell line with a diagnostic label.

03 / Organoids and assembloids

Three-dimensional cultures can introduce aspects of tissue organization and interactions between cell types. Region-specific assembloids are useful for studying migration and circuit development, particularly in developmental disease research. Birey et al. (2017)

04 / Functional validation

Electrophysiology adds evidence about whether neural networks fire, synchronize and respond to perturbation. A disease model becomes more informative when molecular and cellular findings are supported by reproducible functional phenotypes.

What electrophysiology adds

A molecular phenotype does not automatically tell us whether a neural circuit functions differently. MEAs can provide longitudinal measurements of spontaneous firing, bursting, synchronization and stimulus-evoked responses. I am particularly interested in pairing these functional measurements with cell identity, morphology and molecular profiling.

For a disease model, I would want to compare patient and control lines, assess independent differentiations and test whether a functional phenotype is reproducible. Isogenic gene-corrected or mutation-introduced lines can help separate the effect of a variant from differences in genetic background, although they do not remove every source of variability.

05 / What remains difficult?

Aging, vascularization, immune interactions, long-range connectivity and variability are ongoing challenges. For drug exposure and safety assessment, continue to Neuropharmacology & Neurotoxicity.

One central challenge is that many organoids resemble early developmental tissue, whereas diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s often involve aging-related mechanisms. Researchers therefore investigate longer culture, induced aging, cellular stress, co-culture, perfusion and other approaches, each with its own limitations.

06 / Brain organoids in space: why would we do that?

One of the most unexpected approaches to improving disease models is to send human brain organoids into space. Alysson Muotri and collaborators have used the International Space Station (ISS) as an experimental environment to investigate how neural tissue responds to spaceflight and whether disease-related differences become more apparent.

Rett syndrome / MECP2 / 30 days on the ISS

A 2026 Scientific Data publication describes an experiment using iPSC-derived human brain organoids carrying a MECP2 nonsense mutation associated with Rett syndrome and organoids from an unaffected familial control. After 30 days of culture on Earth, a set of organoids spent another 30 days aboard the ISS, while matched controls remained on Earth. The researchers generated mass-spectrometry proteomic profiles covering approximately 6,000 protein groups. Martins et al. (2026)

Rett syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder, not a classic late-onset neurodegenerative disease. It is relevant here because it shows how patient-specific genetic backgrounds can be investigated under a different environmental condition.

What is the scientific question?

Many neural organoid models remain developmentally immature. Researchers are interested in whether the space environment changes maturation, cellular stress pathways or disease-relevant molecular signatures, and whether those responses differ between patient-derived and control organoids.

But spaceflight is not a shortcut that has been proven to reproduce brain aging. Microgravity, radiation, launch and handling conditions can influence cells differently. Faster developmental changes, stress and aging must be distinguished experimentally.

Why could this matter for treatment development?

If a particular molecular pathway changes differently in disease-derived organoids, it may suggest a mechanism to investigate or a candidate target for follow-up studies. The ISS National Laboratory has described how this research program helped motivate investigation of a potential Rett syndrome treatment, including clinical testing. That does not mean an organoid experiment has demonstrated that a treatment works in patients. ISS National Laboratory (2026)

What I'd want to know: Can the molecular differences be reproduced using Earth-based models? Are they accompanied by measurable changes in neural function? And can a candidate intervention rescue the disease-related phenotype, rather than simply alter a stress marker?

This is a conceptual case study of published work, not a space experiment I have performed.

07 / From genetic risk to functional circuits

Another research direction I find compelling is the attempt to connect genetic changes with measurable disturbances in neural network development. A disease-associated variant may alter differentiation, synapses or cellular signaling, but understanding how it changes circuit behavior requires functional measurements.

Tal Sharf / 22q11.2 microdeletion and schizophrenia risk

In 2025, Tal Sharf at UC Santa Cruz received funding to investigate how the 22q11.2 microdeletion, a strong genetic risk factor for schizophrenia, affects developing brain circuits. The announced project combines brain organoids, high-density neuroelectronics, microfluidics and computational approaches, with particular interest in corticothalamic activity and dopamine-related signaling. UC Santa Cruz (2025)

This is an ongoing research direction, not a completed demonstration that an organoid reproduces schizophrenia. What makes it interesting to me is the possibility of following a causal chain from a defined genetic alteration to changes in developing circuit activity.

In practice, I'd want to combine genetic and cellular characterization with electrophysiology, perturbations and appropriate controls. Can an observed network difference be linked to a specific pathway? Can a drug or gene correction restore the phenotype? Does that rescue persist over time?

For more on Sharf's work on emergent activity and closed-loop systems, see Organoid Intelligence.
