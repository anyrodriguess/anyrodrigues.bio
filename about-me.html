how science fiction somehow became a research direction

01 // IT STARTED WITH SCIENCE FICTION

I can trace my interest in bioengineering back to a slightly unusual place: Transcendence.

I watched it before I really knew what bioengineering could be, and became fascinated by its imagined boundary between biology and technology.

The idea of uploading a human mind into a computer was obviously science fiction, but the question underneath it stayed with me:

how far can we actually connect living systems and technology?
[ Transcendence / science-fiction image pending... ]

02 // THEN CAME ORGAN-ON-CHIP

During my Bachelor's in Bioengineering, that question started to feel considerably less fictional.

I discovered organ-on-chip technology, and especially the idea of a patient-on-chip: taking cells from an individual and building experimental systems that reproduce aspects of their biology outside the body.

Suddenly, engineering technology around living biological systems wasn't science fiction at all.

It sounded almost like science fiction again, except this time it was real research.

From there, I knew I wanted my work to stay somewhere around this interface between engineered systems and living biology.

[ organ-on-chip / patient-on-chip image pending... ]

03 // THEN I STARTED LISTENING TO CELLS

That interest eventually brought me to neural electrophysiology.

At DZNE in Dresden, I worked with olfactory bulb–olfactory epithelium neural co-cultures and MEA electrophysiology.

Later, during my Master's research at TU Dresden / CRTD, I worked with adult zebrafish retina on HD-MEAs.

There was something fascinating about placing living neural tissue on a chip and being able to listen to its electrical activity.

But recording was only part of what interested me.

If we can record from a neural network, we can also give it input. We can stimulate it. Change its environment. Observe how it responds.

And eventually ask whether those responses themselves change with experience.

living neural system ⇄ electrophysiology interface

The chip stops being simply a measuring device.

It becomes an interface.

[ MEA / electrophysiology image pending... ]

04 // AND THEN I FOUND ORGANOID INTELLIGENCE

After my Master's, I joined MaxWell Biosystems, where I continued learning about HD-MEA technology and the different biological systems being interfaced with it.

Being surrounded by HD-MEA applications exposed me to a much wider range of neural models and to the growing use of these systems with human brain organoids.

It was there that I first encountered Organoid Intelligence.

The idea immediately connected many of the things that had interested me separately:

organ-on-chip → neural electrophysiology → brain organoids → stimulation → computation

And, unexpectedly, it brought me back to Transcendence.

Not because Organoid Intelligence is about uploading human consciousness into a computer — it isn't.

In some ways, it asks almost the opposite question.

SCIENCE FICTION:

can we recreate biological intelligence in silicon?

THE QUESTION THAT INTERESTS ME NOW:

what happens if we build computational systems around living neural tissue itself?

05 // WHICH BRINGS ME BACK TO THE MOVIE...

[ SPOILER ALERT ]

At the end of Transcendence, we're left with an uncomfortable question.

Was what existed inside the machine really Will?

Was it a copy? An artificial intelligence that believed it was him? Something new?

And perhaps the harder question:

how could anyone know?

That question interests me much more now than it did when I first watched the film.

Because before asking whether a machine — or, for that matter, a biological neural system grown in a laboratory — could ever be conscious, there is a much more fundamental problem:

WHAT IS CONSCIOUSNESS?

How do we distinguish consciousness from intelligence?

From learning?

From memory?

From adaptation?

From a system simply producing increasingly complex responses to its environment?

And what evidence would actually convince us that something other than ourselves was experiencing anything at all?

I don't think Organoid Intelligence answers those questions.

But I find it fascinating that experimental biology is beginning to create systems that force us to ask them more carefully.

06 // SO, NOW...

The movie that once made me curious about an almost absurd science-fiction idea eventually led me, through a very real path of bioengineering, organ-on-chip systems, electrophysiology and HD-MEAs, back to some surprisingly similar questions.

Only now I can approach them experimentally.

Can we give living neural networks structured information?

Can they adapt?

Can they learn?

How would we know?

What does computation mean when the computational substrate is alive?

And where exactly is the boundary between a biological model and a biological information-processing system?

This is where I want to take my research:

brain organoids + HD-MEA electrophysiology + stimulation + biological computation
