MORE ABOUT ME...

how science fiction somehow turned into a research direction

01 // IT STARTED WITH SCIENCE FICTION

One of the first things that made me want to study bioengineering was, perhaps unexpectedly, the movie Transcendence.

The idea that biology and technology could become so deeply connected fascinated me. At the time, it was mostly science fiction to me. I didn't know exactly what kind of research existed at that intersection — I just knew that I wanted to understand it.

02 // THEN CAME ORGAN-ON-CHIP

During my Bachelor's in Bioengineering, I learned about organ-on-chip technology.

What caught my attention even more was the idea of a patient-on-chip: using cells derived from an individual patient to build experimental models that could reproduce aspects of their biology outside the body.

It sounded almost like science fiction again, except this time it was real research.

From there, I knew I wanted my work to stay somewhere around this interface between engineered systems and living biology.

[ organ-on-chip image / drawing pending... ]

03 // I ENDED UP LISTENING TO CELLS

That interest eventually brought me to electrophysiology and microelectrode arrays.

At DZNE in Dresden, I worked with olfactory bulb–olfactory epithelium neural co-cultures and MEA electrophysiology. Later, during my Master's research at TU Dresden / CRTD, I worked with adult zebrafish retina on HD-MEAs.

I became interested not only in recording electrical activity, but in the interaction between a living neural system and the technology underneath it: how we stimulate it, what it responds to, and what its electrical activity can tell us about the way the network processes information.

[ electrophysiology / MEA image pending... ]

04 // HD-MEAs → ORGANOIDS

After my Master's, I joined MaxWell Biosystems, a company working with high-density microelectrode array technology.

Being surrounded by HD-MEA applications exposed me to a much wider range of neural models and to the growing use of these systems with human brain organoids.

It was there that I first learned about Organoid Intelligence.

The idea immediately connected many of the things that had interested me separately: engineered biological systems, neural electrophysiology, brain organoids, stimulation, computation, and the possibility of interacting with living neural networks rather than simply observing them.

05 // NOW

I want to understand what happens when we stop treating neural organoids only as models of the brain and start asking what they can do.

Can we give a living neural network meaningful input? Can it adapt to that input? Can we detect learning through electrophysiology? And what kinds of interfaces do we need in order to create a real closed loop between biological and artificial systems?

This is where I want to take my research next: brain organoids + HD-MEA electrophysiology + stimulation + biological computation.
