SCIENTIFIC INTERESTS / NOTES FROM THE RABBIT HOLE

Brain Organoids

Growing a brain model is one thing. Making it behave like human brain tissue is another.

Brain organoids are three-dimensional neural tissues generated from human stem cells. Under suitable conditions, these cells can self-organize and reproduce selected features of human neural development.

Yet many questions remain about their physiological relevance. How can researchers introduce vascular networks, improve maturation, reproduce interactions between brain regions and obtain reliable functional measurements?

About this page: A collection of scientific papers, research groups and experimental questions concerning the engineering of human neural models. These notes reflect independent reading, not personal experience culturing brain organoids.

📖 01 / model_limitations.txtTHE PROBLEM

01 / What is a brain organoid still missing?

Brain organoids can reproduce selected features of human neural development. Depending on the protocol, they may contain neural progenitors, neurons, astrocytes and other cell populations.

However, they are not miniature, complete human brains. Their architecture, cellular diversity, maturation and physiological environment differ substantially from the human nervous system.

ChallengeWhat researchers are trying
Oxygen and nutrients Endothelial networks, hydrogels, perfusion and microfluidics
Immature cells Long-term culture and improved maturation protocols
Missing brain regions Region-specific organoids and assembloids
Missing immune cells Microglial integration and multicellular co-cultures
Batch variability Standardized differentiation, controlled matrices and quality metrics
Limited functional readouts MEAs, imaging, stimulation and multimodal characterization

Addressing these limitations requires more than stem-cell biology. It also involves materials science, microengineering, electrophysiology and computational analysis.

📖 02 / vascularization.logRESEARCH

02 / How do you give an organoid blood vessels?

In the human brain, blood vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients, remove waste and contribute to the neurovascular unit. Conventional brain organoids lack an equivalent functional circulatory system.

As organoids become larger, cells deep inside the tissue can experience insufficient oxygen and nutrient delivery, contributing to stress and necrosis.

Yale / In-Hyun Park and collaborators — 2019

ETV2 vascular-like networks

Cakir and colleagues engineered cortical organoids containing cells expressing the transcription factor ETV2. These cells contributed to endothelial-like networks within the tissue.

The researchers reported improved functional maturation and features associated with the blood-brain barrier, including tight-junction and transporter markers.

The study also showed that engineered endothelial cells could contribute to perfused vessels after transplantation in vivo.

This was an important step, but vascular-like networks in culture should not automatically be described as a complete, continuously perfused vascular system.

[Cakir et al., 2019]

Galway, CÚRAM and Edinburgh — 2025

endothelial integration hydrogels neurovascular unit

Fumadó Navarro and colleagues used extracellular-matrix-based hydrogels to introduce human brain microvascular endothelial cells into developing cerebral organoids.

The resulting endothelial networks extended into the tissue and displayed features associated with the neurovascular unit, including interactions with astrocyte-like end-feet and pericytes.

The vascularized organoids showed greater medium internalization and up to a threefold reduction in apoptosis compared with non-vascularized controls.

However, the authors identified the absence of luminal perfusion as an important remaining limitation. Vascular integration and functional blood circulation are not interchangeable.

[Fumadó Navarro et al., 2025]

What does vascularization actually mean?

ClaimWhat it demonstrates
Endothelial markers Cells express features associated with endothelial identity
Vascular-like networks Cells form organized vessel-like structures
Barrier characteristics Selected molecular or functional features of a vascular barrier
Perfusion Fluid demonstrably flows through connected luminal structures
Open question: Could combining vascular networks with controlled microfluidic perfusion improve organoid maturation and make electrophysiological recordings more stable over long experimental periods?
📖 03 / maturation.exe2026

03 / What if an organoid needs years to mature?

Human brain development is unusually slow, with many processes associated with neuronal maturation continuing long after birth. This presents a challenge for organoid research: how can researchers study mature human neural biology when many models resemble relatively early developmental stages?

Paola Arlotta's group / Harvard — 2026

long-term culture 5+ years single-cell analysis

Faravelli, Antón-Bolaños and colleagues maintained human cortical organoids in culture for more than five years and investigated how their cells changed over time.

By combining single-cell transcriptomics, epigenetic analysis, structural measurements and functional observations, the team found that organoid cells followed aspects of human developmental timing.

Older neural progenitors retained information about their developmental age. When combined with younger cells, they could produce later neuronal fates without repeating the earlier developmental sequence.

The findings do not mean that five-year-old organoids are equivalent to adult human brains. Nevertheless, they challenge the assumption that organoids can only represent a narrow period of early human development.

[Faravelli et al., 2026]

Open question: How does long-term maturation affect spontaneous activity, stimulus responses, synaptic plasticity and network organization? Could these changes be tracked longitudinally using electrophysiology?

Can gravity influence brain organoid development?

An unexpected experimental variable in organoid engineering is gravity itself.

Research involving neural organoids aboard the International Space Station has suggested that spaceflight conditions can influence neuronal differentiation and maturation. However, whether these changes produce more functionally mature neural networks remains unclear.

OrganoTherapeutics is exploring related questions using a Random Positioning Machine to simulate microgravity. The company is studying patient-derived midbrain organoids carrying a GBA mutation associated with Parkinson's disease, investigating viability, differentiation and disease-related phenotypes.

Its research plans also include automated organoid-on-chip experiments in orbit. Simulated microgravity on Earth and actual spaceflight conditions should not be treated as equivalent experimental environments.

Changes in developmental or maturation markers do not necessarily demonstrate improved neural circuit function.

Further reading: Marotta et al., 2024 · OrganoTherapeutics — microgravity experiments

📖 04 / assembloids.txtCONNECTIVITY

04 / What happens when we connect different brain regions?

A cortical organoid can reproduce selected properties of cortical development, but the brain does not consist of one isolated region.

Different neural populations communicate across regions. Some neurons migrate long distances during development, and functional circuits depend on these interactions.

Sergiu Paşca's laboratory / Stanford

assembloids cell migration circuit formation

Paşca and colleagues pioneered approaches that combine region-specific neural organoids into assembloids.

In a landmark 2017 study, Birey and colleagues fused forebrain spheroids representing different neural populations and observed interneuron migration between the structures.

Subsequent work developed models of cortico-striatal and cortico-motor connections, allowing researchers to investigate selected features of human circuit formation.

In 2025, the group reported midline assembloids for studying regulators of human axon guidance.

Assembloids shift the experimental question from generating individual cell populations to examining whether those populations can establish meaningful connections and communication.

[Birey et al., 2017]

[Onesto et al., 2025]

Not a complete brain: Assembloids are controlled models of selected interactions. Connecting two organoids does not reproduce the full circuitry of the human nervous system.
📖 05 / missing_cells.txtMICROGLIA

05 / What about the brain's immune cells?

Microglia contribute to brain development, synaptic remodeling, immune surveillance and responses to injury.

Many neural organoid protocols do not reliably generate the full range of non-neuronal cells present in human brain tissue.

Adding microglia to the model

iPSC-derived microglia multicellular models

A 2025 study published in the European Journal of Neuroscience investigated how human iPSC-derived microglia integrate into cerebral organoids and develop more in-vivo-like characteristics.

Other researchers are studying microglia-containing organoids as models of early-life immune challenges and neuroinflammation.

Such models may help investigate how immune signaling influences neuronal maturation, network function and neurodegenerative processes.

[Wogram et al., 2025]

[Buonfiglioli et al., 2025]

Open question: How does microglial integration influence spontaneous neural activity and responses to inflammatory stimulation?
📖 06 / organoid_on_chip.exeENGINEERING

06 / Can microfluidics make organoids more useful?

Organoid-on-chip systems combine three-dimensional biological models with engineered microenvironments.

Microfluidic devices can control fluid delivery, exposure timing, gradients and interactions between tissue compartments.

These properties are relevant to pharmacology and toxicology, where compound concentration and exposure duration can influence experimental results.

A breakthrough: perfusing neural tissue through 3D microfluidics

3D soft microfluidics perfusion hydrogel engineering

In 2023, Grebenyuk, Ranga and collaborators at KU Leuven reported a soft, three-dimensional microfluidic system built using printed, capillary-scale hydrogel channels.

By perfusing engineered neural tissue through these networks, the researchers improved oxygen and nutrient delivery, reduced core hypoxia and supported neural differentiation. The study also investigated perfusion of cortical organoids.

This was an important engineering advance because the channels delivered culture medium within thick tissue rather than exclusively around its external surface.

However, these channels were synthetic microfluidic structures, not biological endothelial blood vessels. The study did not establish a mature blood-brain barrier or demonstrate learning-related neural network function.

[Grebenyuk et al., 2023]

What makes an organoid-on-chip platform experimentally useful?

  • Controlled delivery of nutrients and compounds
  • Monitoring oxygen and environmental conditions
  • Defined interactions between tissue compartments
  • Optical access for imaging
  • Compatibility with electrical stimulation and MEA recording
  • Longitudinal measurements of the same tissue

These are potential platform capabilities, not properties that every organoid-on-chip system necessarily possesses.

Placing an organoid inside a microfluidic chip does not automatically generate biological blood vessels or a functional blood-brain barrier. Similarly, fluid flowing around an organoid is not equivalent to perfusion through connected luminal structures within the tissue.

An experimental question: Could a system combining vascularization, controlled perfusion and longitudinal electrophysiology establish whether improved nutrient delivery produces measurable changes in neural activity and maturation?
📖 07 / research_community.directoryLABS

07 / Research groups shaping brain organoid engineering

Different research groups are addressing the limitations of brain organoids through vascularization, long-term maturation, circuit assembly, immune-cell integration and microengineering.

In-Hyun Park and collaborators / Yale

Engineering endothelial-like networks within cortical organoids and investigating neurovascular features. [Paper]

Mihai Lomora, Abhay Pandit and collaborators / Galway, CÚRAM and Edinburgh

Developing biomaterial-based approaches to endothelial integration, neurovascular-unit features and reduced cell death within cerebral organoids. [Paper]

Paola Arlotta and collaborators / Harvard and Broad Institute

Investigating human cortical development, cell-type diversity, long-term organoid maturation and developmental timing. [Paper]

Sergiu Paşca / Stanford

Developing region-specific organoids and assembloids to investigate neuronal migration, axon guidance, circuit formation and scalable human neural models. [Recent paper]

Wogram and collaborators / microglial integration

Investigating the integration of human iPSC-derived microglia into cerebral organoids and the resulting microglial phenotype. Related work by Buonfiglioli and collaborators examines early-life immune challenges using microglia-containing cerebral organoids. [Paper] [Related paper]

Adrian Ranga and collaborators / KU Leuven

Combining 3D hydrogel engineering, synthetic capillary-scale microfluidics and long-term perfusion to support larger neural tissue constructs.

Their work connects tissue architecture, oxygen and nutrient delivery, and neural differentiation. It illustrates how several engineering approaches can be integrated into a single experimental platform.

Nevertheless, biological vascular remodeling, mature blood-brain barrier function and reproducible neural circuit performance remain distinct challenges. [Paper]

A shared challenge: Across these approaches, an important next step is determining whether improvements in tissue structure, oxygenation and survival also produce reproducible improvements in neural circuit function.
📖 08 / research_questions.txtOPEN QUESTIONS

08 / Open questions and future directions

The engineering of brain organoids raises questions that extend beyond morphology and gene expression. How can improvements in tissue complexity be linked to measurable physiological function?

Does vascularization improve network maturation? How does microglial integration influence responses to inflammatory stimuli? Can assembloids develop reproducible communication between neural regions?

And how should researchers determine whether a more complex model is genuinely more informative, rather than simply more difficult to reproduce?

The broader question: How can the biological complexity of human neural organoids be combined with the experimental control and functional measurements required for reproducible research?
