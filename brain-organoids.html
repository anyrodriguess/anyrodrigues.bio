SCIENTIFIC INTERESTS / NOTES FROM THE RABBIT HOLE

Brain Organoids

Growing a brain model is one thing. Making it behave like human brain tissue is another.

Brain organoids are fascinating because human stem cells can self-organize into three-dimensional neural tissue. But the more I read about them, the more interested I become in everything these models are still missing.

Blood vessels, mature cell populations, interactions between brain regions, immune cells, controlled microenvironments, and reliable functional readouts. How are researchers solving these problems?

About this page: My collection of papers, laboratories and questions about engineering better human brain models. This is independent reading, not a claim that I have personally cultured brain organoids.

📖 01 / model_limitations.txt THE PROBLEM

01 / What is a brain organoid still missing?

Brain organoids can reproduce selected features of human neural development. Depending on the differentiation protocol, they may contain neural progenitors, neurons, astrocytes and other cell types.

But they are not miniature, complete human brains. Their architecture, cellular diversity, maturation and physiological environment differ substantially from the human nervous system.

Challenge What researchers are trying
Oxygen and nutrients Endothelial networks, hydrogels, perfusion and microfluidics
Immature cells Long-term culture, improved media and maturation protocols
Missing brain regions Region-specific organoids and assembloids
Missing immune cells Microglial integration and multicellular co-cultures
Batch variability Standardized differentiation, controlled matrices and quality metrics
Limited functional readouts MEAs, imaging, stimulation and multimodal characterization

What I find particularly interesting is that these problems cannot be solved by stem-cell biology alone. They also require engineering, materials science, electrophysiology and computational analysis.

📖 02 / vascularization.log RESEARCH

02 / How do you give an organoid blood vessels?

This is a major engineering challenge in the field.

In the human brain, blood vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients, remove waste and participate in the neurovascular unit. A conventional brain organoid growing in culture medium has no equivalent functional circulatory system.

As organoids become larger, cells deep inside the tissue can experience insufficient oxygen and nutrient delivery, contributing to stress and necrosis.

Yale / In-Hyun Park and collaborators — 2019

ETV2 vascular-like networks

Cakir and colleagues engineered cortical organoids with cells expressing the transcription factor ETV2. These cells contributed to endothelial-like networks within the tissue.

The researchers reported improved functional maturation and features associated with the blood-brain barrier, including tight-junction and transporter markers.

Their study also demonstrated that the engineered endothelial cells could support perfused vessels after transplantation in vivo.

This was an important step, but vascular-like networks in culture should not automatically be described as a complete, continuously perfused vascular system.

[Cakir et al., 2019]

Galway, CÚRAM and Edinburgh — 2025

endothelial integration hydrogels neurovascular unit

A 2025 study by Fumadó Navarro and colleagues took a different approach.

Researchers from the University of Galway, CÚRAM and the University of Edinburgh used extracellular-matrix-based hydrogels to deliver human brain microvascular endothelial cells to developing cerebral organoids.

The resulting endothelial networks extended into the organoid tissue and displayed features associated with the neurovascular unit, including interactions with astrocyte-like end-feet and pericytes.

The vascularized organoids showed greater medium internalization and up to a threefold reduction in apoptosis compared with non-vascularized controls.

That's a particularly interesting result: engineering the surrounding tissue environment changed how well cells survived inside the model.

However, the authors also identified the absence of luminal perfusion as an important remaining limitation. Vascular integration and functional blood circulation are not interchangeable.

[Fumadó Navarro et al., 2025]

What does vascularization actually mean?

Claim What it demonstrates
Endothelial markers Cells express features associated with endothelial identity
Vascular-like networks Cells form organized vessel-like structures
Barrier characteristics Selected molecular or functional features of a vascular barrier
Perfusion Fluid demonstrably flows through connected luminal structures
Open question: Could combining vascular networks with controlled microfluidic perfusion improve organoid maturation and make electrophysiological recordings more stable over long experimental periods?
📖 03 / maturation.exe 2026

03 / What if an organoid needs years to mature?

Human brain development is unusually slow. Many processes associated with neuronal maturation continue long after birth.

That creates a difficult problem for organoid research: how do we study mature human neural biology when our models often resemble relatively early developmental stages?

Paola Arlotta's group / Harvard — 2026

long-term culture 5+ years single-cell analysis

In August 2026, Faravelli, Antón-Bolaños and colleagues published a remarkable study in Nature.

They maintained human cortical organoids in culture for more than five years and investigated how their cells changed over time.

By combining single-cell transcriptomics, epigenetic analysis, structural measurements and functional observations, the team found that organoid cells followed aspects of human developmental timing.

Particularly fascinating was the finding that older neural progenitors retained information about their developmental age. When combined with younger cells, they could produce later neuronal fates without repeating the earlier sequence.

The work does not mean that five-year-old organoids are equivalent to adult human brains. Neuronal populations still changed over time, and the models remained incomplete.

But it challenges the assumption that organoids can only represent a narrow period of early human development.

[Faravelli et al., 2026]

Open question: How does long-term maturation change spontaneous activity, stimulus responses, synaptic plasticity and network organization? And could we track those changes repeatedly using electrophysiology?

Can gravity influence brain organoid development?

One unexpected variable in organoid engineering is gravity itself. Research involving brain organoids aboard the International Space Station has suggested that spaceflight conditions can influence neuronal differentiation and maturation. However, whether these changes produce more functionally mature neural networks remains unclear.

OrganoTherapeutics is exploring this from another perspective. Using a Random Positioning Machine to simulate microgravity, the company is studying patient-derived midbrain organoids carrying a GBA mutation associated with Parkinson's disease. Their experiments investigate cell viability, differentiation and disease-related phenotypes before moving toward an automated organoid-on-chip experiment in orbit.

Gravity is another experimental variable alongside culture conditions, stimulation and tissue architecture.

Open question: Could changing the physical environment influence not only how neural tissue develops, but also how its circuits organize and function?

📖 04 / assembloids.txt CONNECTIVITY

04 / What happens when we connect different brain regions?

A cortical organoid can reproduce selected properties of cortical development, but the brain does not consist of one isolated region.

Different neural populations communicate across regions. Some neurons migrate long distances during development, and functional circuits depend on these interactions.

Sergiu Paşca's laboratory / Stanford

assembloids cell migration circuit formation

Paşca and colleagues pioneered approaches that combine region-specific neural organoids into what they call assembloids.

In a landmark 2017 study, Birey and colleagues fused forebrain spheroids representing different neural populations and observed interneuron migration between the structures.

Subsequent work developed models of cortico-striatal and cortico-motor connections, allowing researchers to investigate selected features of human circuit formation.

In 2025, the group also reported midline assembloids for studying regulators of human axon guidance.

I find assembloids especially interesting because they shift the question from "Can we generate the right cells?" to "Can these cells connect and communicate in a meaningful way?"

[Birey et al., 2017]

[Onesto et al., 2025]

Not a complete brain: Assembloids are controlled experimental models of selected interactions. Connecting two organoids does not reproduce the full circuitry of the human nervous system.
📖 05 / missing_cells.txt MICROGLIA

05 / What about the brain's immune cells?

Microglia are central to brain development, synaptic remodeling, immune surveillance and responses to injury.

Yet many neural organoid protocols do not reliably generate the full range of non-neuronal cells found in human brain tissue.

Adding microglia to the model

iPSC-derived microglia multicellular models

A 2025 study published in the European Journal of Neuroscience investigated how human iPSC-derived microglia integrate into cerebral organoids and develop more in-vivo-like characteristics.

Other researchers are studying microglia-containing organoids as models of early-life immune challenges and neuroinflammation.

These models could become particularly relevant for understanding how immune signaling influences neuronal maturation, network function and neurodegeneration.

[Wogram et al., 2025]

[Buonfiglioli et al., 2025]

Open question: If we add microglia to a neural organoid, how does that change its spontaneous electrical activity or response to inflammatory stimulation?
📖 06 / organoid_on_chip.exe ENGINEERING

06 / Can microfluidics make organoids more useful?

Organoid-on-chip systems combine three-dimensional biological models with engineered microenvironments.

Microfluidic devices can help researchers control fluid delivery, exposure timing, gradients and interactions between tissue compartments.

This is especially interesting for neuropharmacology and neurotoxicity, where the concentration and duration of an exposure can strongly influence experimental results.

What makes an organoid-on-chip platform experimentally useful?

  • Controlled delivery of nutrients and compounds
  • Monitoring of oxygen and environmental conditions
  • Defined interactions between tissue compartments
  • Optical access for imaging
  • Electrical stimulation and MEA recording
  • Longitudinal measurements of the same tissue

But placing an organoid inside a chip does not automatically create blood vessels or a functional blood-brain barrier. Those properties must be engineered and experimentally demonstrated.

Likewise, fluid flowing around an organoid is not the same as fluid flowing through a connected vascular network inside it.

An experimental question: A vascularized brain organoid with controlled perfusion and longitudinal electrophysiology, allowing researchers to study how changes in nutrient delivery affect neural activity and maturation.

📖 08 / personal_notes.txt MY QUESTIONS

08 / Open questions and future directions

My experience has mainly been with electrophysiology and neural preparations rather than brain organoid culture.

That is probably why I keep thinking about the functional side of these models. We can characterize their cell populations, morphology and gene expression, but what can their electrical activity tell us?

How does vascularization affect network maturation? Would organoids with microglia respond differently to inflammatory stimuli? Can assembloids develop reproducible communication between regions?

And how do we decide when a more complex model is genuinely better, rather than simply more difficult to reproduce?

The bigger question: How can we combine the biological complexity of human organoids with the experimental control and functional measurements needed for reliable research?
