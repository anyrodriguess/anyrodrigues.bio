Biological Computation

What happens when the computer is alive?

We have built machines capable of performing quintillions of calculations per second. Meanwhile, the human brain continuously processes information, learns and adapts using roughly the power of a small light bulb.

Could living neurons teach us a different way to compute? And what happens when we connect them directly to silicon?

About this page: My personal collection of experiments, research groups, companies and questions about biological computing. These are interests and independent reading notes, not claims of personal experience with all the systems discussed.

📖 01 / brain_vs_supercomputer.exe ENERGY

01 / Is the brain a better computer?

It depends entirely on what we ask it to do. A conventional computer is exceptionally good at precise, repeatable calculations. The brain is remarkable at integrating sensory information, controlling movement, adapting to unfamiliar situations and learning from experience.

The surprising part is how little energy the brain uses. Its metabolic power consumption is often estimated at approximately 20 watts.

Compare that with the world's fastest supercomputer in the June 2026 TOP500 ranking.

HUMAN BRAIN ~20 W Estimated metabolic power
LINESHINE / 2026 2.198 EFLOP/s Measured LINPACK performance
LINESHINE / POWER 42.22 MW TOP500 listed power

LineShine, installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, China, achieved approximately 2.198 exaFLOP/s on the LINPACK benchmark. Its listed power consumption was 42,220 kilowatts.

That's more than two million times the brain's approximate 20-watt power consumption. But it would be misleading to say the brain is two million times more efficient.

Important distinction: FLOPS measure floating-point calculations. They do not measure intelligence, consciousness, perception or learning. A human brain cannot be meaningfully benchmarked against a supercomputer using LINPACK.

So what makes neural processing interesting?

In conventional computing, memory and processing are often physically separated. Moving information between them has an energy and time cost.

In biological neural networks, information processing and changes associated with memory occur within interconnected cells and synapses. Activity is sparse in many circuits, and the network itself can change through experience.

This is one reason researchers are interested in neuromorphic computing, artificial synapses and living-neuron computing.

Question I keep coming back to: If biological neural networks can adapt without being explicitly programmed for every situation, could that ability be useful in a computer?

📖 02 / ai_energy_report.txt 2026

02 / AI has an energy problem

The growth of artificial intelligence has made computational energy demand increasingly difficult to ignore.

Training large models requires substantial computing resources. Running them also consumes electricity, especially when millions of people use AI services and increasingly demanding applications become common.

According to the International Energy Agency's 2026 outlook, global data-centre electricity consumption was approximately 485 TWh in 2025. Its central projection reaches around 950 TWh in 2030.

DATA CENTRES / 2025 485 TWh IEA estimate
DATA CENTRES / 2030 950 TWh IEA central projection
GROWTH ~2× Projected total electricity demand

Not all data-centre electricity is used for AI. However, AI-focused facilities are expected to account for a rapidly growing share of demand.

There is also an interesting paradox: an individual AI operation can become more energy efficient while total electricity consumption increases because the technology is being used much more widely.

Could biological computing help?

Possibly, but that remains to be demonstrated. Living neurons need a controlled environment, nutrients, temperature regulation, recording electronics and supporting computers.

A fair energy comparison must include the entire experimental platform, not just the electrical activity of the neurons.

My question: Could biological computing become genuinely energy efficient at the system level, or is the more realistic opportunity to learn new computational principles from neurons?

📖 03 / living_neurons.exe HOW IT WORKS

03 / How do you turn neurons into a computer?

The basic idea is surprisingly close to electrophysiology. Cultured neurons can generate electrical signals, respond to stimulation and form interconnected networks.

Microelectrode arrays provide an interface between these biological networks and digital electronics.

  1. Input: a digital system converts information into patterns of electrical stimulation.
  2. Biological processing: neurons respond through their collective network dynamics.
  3. Output: electrodes record activity such as spikes and network responses.
  4. Feedback: software interprets the response and changes subsequent stimulation.

Where does learning enter the picture?

Neural networks can change their responses with repeated activity. Synaptic plasticity, including activity-dependent changes in connection strength, is one of the mechanisms that makes biological neural systems interesting.

However, not every change in firing is evidence of learning. Adaptation, fatigue, changes in excitability and recording variability can also alter neural responses.

To demonstrate learning convincingly, experiments need carefully designed tasks, controls and reproducible evidence of improved performance or retained information.

Why MEAs matter: They allow researchers to observe and stimulate neural activity without relying exclusively on destructive endpoint measurements. They are an essential interface in many experimental biological computing systems.
📖 04 / experiments.log CASE STUDIES

04 / The experiments that made me pay attention

DishBrain — playing Pong with neurons

2022 2D neuronal cultures closed-loop feedback

In 2022, Kagan and colleagues reported an experiment in which living neurons cultured on an electrode array interacted with a simplified version of Pong.

The system translated the position of the ball into electrical stimulation and used recorded neural activity to control the paddle.

The authors reported changes in performance associated with structured feedback. The experiment raised fascinating questions about adaptation and goal-directed behavior in cultured neural networks.

But DishBrain used two-dimensional neuronal cultures, not cerebral organoids. And the interpretation of learning in such systems remains an active scientific discussion.

[Kagan et al., 2022]

Brainoware — a brain organoid as a computational reservoir

2023 3D brain organoid HD-MEA

A different approach was demonstrated by Cai and colleagues in a 2023 Nature Electronics paper.

Their Brainoware system used a brain organoid connected to a high-density microelectrode array. Electrical stimulation supplied information, while the organoid's responses were used in a reservoir-computing framework.

The researchers explored tasks including speech recognition and prediction of nonlinear equations.

This is particularly interesting because the biological tissue is not simply a model of the brain. Its dynamics become part of the computational process.

It is still a research demonstration, not a general-purpose biological computer.

[Cai et al., 2023]

Something I find fascinating: DishBrain and Brainoware use different biological preparations and computational strategies. How much of their behavior comes from the living network, and how much comes from the surrounding software?
📖 05 / companies.directory INDUSTRY

05 / Who is building computers with living neurons?

Several companies are now exploring the interface between living neural tissue and computing. But they are not all building the same thing.

Cortical Labs

Living neuronal cultures Bio-digital hardware

Cortical Labs is associated with the DishBrain research and develops systems that combine cultured neurons, electrode arrays and digital interfaces.

Its CL1 platform is designed around living neural networks integrated with electronic hardware. Cortical Cloud explores remote access to biological computing experiments.

FinalSpark

Human neural organoids Remote Neuroplatform

FinalSpark develops an experimental Neuroplatform using living human neural tissue, including organoids.

Its work explores remote experimentation, stimulation, electrophysiological recording and the possibility of using organoid networks for computation.

The team published a technical description of the Neuroplatform in 2024.

[Jordan et al., 2024]

The Biological Computing Co.

Living-neuron experiments Neuron-derived AI software

The Biological Computing Co. takes a particularly interesting approach. The company studies living neurons to discover computational principles that can improve AI.

In September 2026, it announced a collaboration with AWS involving a neuron-derived video generation model.

Importantly, the resulting commercial software runs on conventional computing infrastructure. It does not require living neurons to be present when a user generates a video.

This is a different direction from using living tissue as the computing hardware itself.

Read the company's September 2026 announcement ↗

Three approaches, three different questions

Company Approach
Cortical Labs Living neurons integrated with electronic computing systems
FinalSpark Organoid-based experimental computing platform
The Biological Computing Co. Using living-neuron research to develop computational software
A distinction worth keeping: A computer containing living neurons is not the same thing as software inspired by, or developed using measurements from, living neurons. Company claims about speed or energy savings also need independent benchmarking.
📖 06 / research_community.txt LABS

06 / The research community behind the idea

Biological computing isn't the work of one company or one laboratory. It draws on electrophysiology, stem-cell biology, neuroscience, engineering and computer science.

Kagan and colleagues — DishBrain

Their 2022 work brought together cultured neurons, electrophysiology and a digital feedback environment. It helped bring closed-loop in-vitro neural systems into a wider discussion about computation.

Cai and colleagues — Brainoware

This research demonstrated how brain organoids and HD-MEAs could be combined with reservoir computing to perform experimental tasks.

Jordan and colleagues — FinalSpark Neuroplatform

Their work focused on building an accessible experimental platform for organoid-based computation, including stimulation, recording and remote research access.

The wider organoid intelligence community

Researchers working on organoid intelligence are also investigating how human neural models, electrophysiology, plasticity and bio-digital interfaces might contribute to future computational systems.

For the broader questions about learning, intelligence and the interpretation of neural responses, I keep separate notes in Organoid Intelligence .

What I'd like to see more of: Experiments that compare different neural preparations under the same stimulation protocols and computational benchmarks. How much do cell composition, maturation, network architecture and electrode density matter?
📖 07 / limitations.txt OPEN QUESTIONS

07 / Why aren't biological computers everywhere?

Because turning an exciting experimental demonstration into reliable computing hardware is an enormous challenge.

  • Reproducibility: two neural cultures or organoids may behave differently even under similar conditions.
  • Longevity: living systems require maintenance and may change over time.
  • Scalability: increasing the number of neurons does not automatically increase useful computing performance.
  • Readout: electrical recordings capture only part of the network's activity.
  • Benchmarking: researchers need fair ways to compare biological and silicon-based systems.
  • Energy efficiency: the entire platform must be included in the calculation.
  • Ethics: increasingly complex human neural models raise questions about oversight, responsible use and possible future moral considerations.

These challenges are also what make the field scientifically interesting. There is still so much we don't understand about the relationship between neural activity, adaptation and computation.

📖 08 / personal_notes.txt MY QUESTIONS

08 / Why I keep coming back to this

My background is in neural electrophysiology, particularly recording activity using microelectrode arrays.

I am used to thinking about how experimental conditions affect firing patterns and network responses. Biological computing adds a new dimension to that: what if we use those responses as part of an interactive system?

I'm especially curious about how researchers distinguish genuine learning from simpler changes in neural excitability, and how experimental design influences that interpretation.

I also wonder whether biological computing will ultimately become a practical computing technology or whether its greatest contribution will be helping us understand the principles of neural information processing.

One question I'd love to investigate: If we give two neural networks the same task but change their architecture, maturity or stimulation pattern, what determines which network adapts more successfully?
