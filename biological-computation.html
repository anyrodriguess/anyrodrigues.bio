Biological Computation

When living neurons become part of a computer

The difference between biological experiments, hybrid systems and general-purpose computing.

01 / Why compute with living neurons?

One reason I keep returning to biological computing is the energy question. A human brain uses roughly 20 watts of metabolic power while continually integrating sensory signals, guiding movement and adapting through experience. Conventional computers excel at accurate, repeatable calculations, but neurons combine information processing and changing connections in the same physical network.

That does not make brains universally better than computers. It makes them interestingly different. Could some of those properties become useful in hybrid computing systems?

02 / A brain and a supercomputer are not running the same task

The June 2026 TOP500 ranking reports the following LINPACK benchmark results. These are measurements of numerical computing performance, not intelligence.

LineShine's reported electrical power is roughly 2.1 million times the brain's metabolic power. That is a comparison of power budgets, not evidence that the brain outperforms the supercomputer. The brain does not run LINPACK, and there is no agreed conversion from cognition into FLOP/s.

The question I find more useful: For which specific tasks might adaptive neural tissue be competitive once we count the power needed to keep the whole experimental system running?

03 / AI is making the energy question harder to ignore

AI models run on conventional computing infrastructure, and their growth is contributing to rising data-centre electricity demand. The International Energy Agency projects global data-centre consumption rising from about 485 TWh in 2025 to around 950 TWh in 2030. These figures describe all data centres, not AI alone. IEA analysis ↗

There is a paradox here: individual AI operations can become more efficient even as total electricity use grows because applications, demand and model sizes change. Biological computing is not a proven solution to this problem. But it is one reason researchers are interested in alternative architectures, alongside neuromorphic chips and more efficient conventional hardware.

04 / What does a living computer actually look like?

In one approach, living neurons grow on a microelectrode array (MEA). Software sends patterns of electrical stimulation to the culture and records its electrical responses. Those responses can be interpreted as signals in a digital task, creating a closed loop: input, neuronal activity, feedback, and another input.

Other approaches use three-dimensional neural organoids rather than two-dimensional cultures. They may offer different cell compositions and tissue organization, but also introduce challenges involving access, reproducibility, maturation and maintenance.

Learning is the difficult part to demonstrate. A changing firing pattern might indicate plasticity, habituation, altered excitability or instability. Showing useful computation requires controlled tasks, suitable comparisons and repeatable benchmarks.

05 / The experiments that made this tangible

DishBrain: neurons playing a simplified Pong task

Kagan and colleagues connected two-dimensional neuronal cultures to a simplified Pong environment and reported changes in activity during closed-loop feedback. The study is intriguing, but it did not involve a three-dimensional brain organoid, nor does it establish human-like cognition. Kagan et al. (2022)

Brainoware: an organoid in a computing pipeline

Guo and colleagues explored a hybrid system using a brain organoid as a physical reservoir for signal-processing tasks. This is distinct from the DishBrain experiment: the experimental architecture and definition of successful computation differ. Guo et al. (2023)

These projects are useful precisely because they show how many different things people mean by biological computation.

I want to follow both the companies and the academic labs behind the publications. A platform that physically computes with living neurons is not the same as software whose design was inspired by experiments with neurons.

07 / What still needs to be demonstrated?

  • Task performance: Can a biological system outperform a relevant digital baseline on a clearly defined task?
  • Reproducibility: Will different cultures behave similarly enough for practical use?
  • Lifetime and stability: How do we keep a living system functional over time?
  • Total energy: What happens when we include incubators, perfusion, electronics, computing and cooling?
  • Interpretation: When does adaptation count as learning rather than a nonspecific physiological response?
My question: Is the biggest near-term contribution of living neural computers likely to be efficient computation, or will these systems first teach us how neural networks learn and adapt?

My interest comes from working with neural electrophysiology: designing stimulation, recording activity and trying to understand what changes in the signal actually mean. For the broader organoid-intelligence discussion, see Organoid Intelligence.
